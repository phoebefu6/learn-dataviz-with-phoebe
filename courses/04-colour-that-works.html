Colour that works

Colour is where charts are usually decided and it is the part of this discipline with the least judgement in it. Whether two series can be told apart by a colour-blind reader is arithmetic. Whether a label is readable is arithmetic. The only genuinely creative decision is which of four jobs the colour is doing, and once that is named, everything downstream follows. Tonight: the four jobs, one rule each, and a real dichromacy transform showing two hues that sit 22.9 apart for you sitting 9.5 apart for somebody else.

Part 1 · covers the four jobs

Four jobs, and one rule for each

6 min live

Almost every colour mistake in a chart is a job mismatch: a categorical palette used for magnitude, or a rainbow used for anything at all.

LiveOne series, one colour4 min▶

Four bars showing revenue for four roasts is one series. It is one measure, measured four times. Giving each bar its own hue is colour-by-rank, and it is probably the most widespread colour mistake in business charts because every spreadsheet tool does it by default.

What it costs:

  • It implies a distinction that is not in the data. The reader looks for what the blue ones have in common. There is nothing.
  • It spends your categorical palette on a chart that needed none of it, so when a genuinely categorical chart appears next to it, the same hues now mean something else.
  • It makes the legend necessary where a direct label would have done, and legends are a matching exercise.

The rule: colour encodes a difference that exists in the data. If the only difference between your bars is their value, the value is already encoded - by length - and colour has nothing left to do. One hue, and spend the attention on the labels.

That pair is one of the bench's own series colours against a white surface: it comes out around 2.1 to 1, below the 3:1 floor. That is not a reason to reject the hue - it is a reason it must carry a direct label rather than relying on a legend, which is precisely what the bench's chart does. A contrast warning is an obligation, not a veto.

Part 2 · covers colour vision

Compute it, do not judge it

6 min live

Roughly one man in twelve has some form of colour-vision deficiency. You cannot check for it by looking, because you are not the person who cannot see it - and the check is a matrix multiplication that takes a millisecond.

LiveNever meaning in hue alone4 min▶

Even a palette that passes needs a second channel, for four reasons that have nothing to do with each other:

  • Colour vision. The measured case above.
  • Print. Somebody will print it in greyscale, and hue collapses to lightness.
  • Forced colours. High-contrast operating-system modes replace your palette entirely.
  • Projection. A dim projector in a bright room flattens everything, and you will be standing next to it.

The second channels, in order of how cheap they are: a direct label (free, and better than a legend anyway), position (a sorted order the reader can rely on), texture (a hatch at 45 degrees), and shape for point marks.

The status-colour rule

Good, warning, serious, critical are reserved and always ship with an icon and a word. A red dot on its own is meaningless to a protanope, in greyscale, and to anybody who has not learnt your convention - which is most people, most of the time.

Self-studySequential and diverging, properly4 min▶
  • Sequential is one hue, light to dark. Lightness carries the magnitude, which is why it survives greyscale and colour blindness for free. A two-hue "sequential" ramp is a diverging palette that has not admitted it.
  • Never a rainbow for magnitude. It has no order anybody can read without a legend, it invents visible boundaries where the data is smooth, and the yellow band reads as a peak wherever it lands. It persists because it looks like more information.
  • Diverging is two hues plus a neutral, and the neutral must sit at the value that genuinely means zero - break-even, no change, the target. A diverging palette centred on the mean of your data is telling a story about your sample, not about the world.
  • Check the number of steps. Beyond about seven, people cannot map a colour back to a bin. If you need more resolution than that, you need a different form or a tooltip.
Build-along

Measure a real palette

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Go back to the bench in session 3 for the vision modes and the palette strip - it is the same widget and the same measurements. Tonight you point them at your own colours.

Name the job. For each use of colour in your chart: identity, magnitude, polarity or state. If you cannot name one, the colour is decoration and it should go.

Cut the hues. One series gets one colour. Count how many charts in your deck are using four hues to say one thing.

Measure. Contrast against your actual surface, and separation under deuteranopia and protanopia. Never judge this by looking.

Second channel. Wherever colour carries meaning, add a label, a position, a texture or a shape. Cheapest first: direct labels are free and better than a legend.

Reserve status. Good, warning, serious, critical are not available as series colours. Take them out of the categorical set and give them an icon and a word.

Open the bench in session 3 and cycle the four vision modes. Watch the palette strip converge and the ΔE drop from 22.9 to about 10. That is a validated palette behaving well - a bad one collapses to single digits.

Run your own two closest hues through the contrast checker above. Text against your real surface, not against white if your surface is not white.

Find a chart of yours using four hues for one measure. Change it to one hue and look at the two versions side by side. Most people find the single-hue version looks more professional, which is a pleasant surprise given it was also the correct one.

Find a rainbow. A heatmap, a map, a risk matrix. Replace it with one hue light-to-dark and check whether anything was lost. It will not have been.

Find a diverging palette and check its midpoint. Is the neutral at the value that genuinely means zero, or at the middle of the data range? The second one is extremely common and it makes the chart a story about your sample.

Add one second channel. Pick the chart where colour is doing the most work and give it direct labels. Then remove the legend, and notice the chart got simpler.

Audit your status colours. Are any of them also serving as series colours anywhere? If so, red means two things in your product and one of them is an emergency.

If you take one thing from tonight. Never decide by looking whether a palette is colour-blind safe. You are structurally the wrong person to judge it, the check is a matrix multiplication, and the bench does it in a millisecond.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Four bars show revenue for four roasts. How many colours?

The only thing distinguishing the bars is their value, and the value is already encoded by length. Four hues imply a categorical distinction that does not exist, spend the categorical palette on a chart that needed none of it, and force a legend where a direct label would do.

2 · Your palette measures ΔE 22.9 under normal vision and 9.5 under protanopia. What does that mean?

9.5 clears the floor of 8, so the palette is usable. It is also less than half the separation most readers get, which is exactly the situation where a direct label or a texture costs nothing and removes the risk. And roughly one man in twelve has some form of CVD, which is not a rounding error.

3 · Why is a rainbow palette wrong for magnitude?

Magnitude needs an ordered channel, and lightness is one - which is why sequential is a single hue from light to dark. Hue is not ordered: nobody can say whether green is more than orange without checking. The yellow band also reads as a peak wherever it happens to fall.
